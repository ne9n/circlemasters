Every summer, tens of thousands of aviation enthusiasts descend upon Oshkosh, Wisconsin, for EAA AirVenture. Among the roar of warbirds, modern aerobatic performers, and endless rows of experimental aircraft, a very special corner of Pioneer Airport buzzes with the pure, unadulterated excitement of flight.

Here at KidVenture, where young attendees build wooden wing ribs, rivet sheet metal, and test flight simulators, the Control Line (CL) Flight Circles deliver something truly magical: the visceral, unforgettable experience of commanding an airplane in real flight with their own hands.

Holding a control handle tethered to dual steel lines, a child feels the mechanical pull of lift, the surge of electric brushless power, and the instantaneous response of the elevator. Over the past three decades, this volunteer-driven program has introduced more than 25,000 young pilots to the thrill of flight.

1. The Roots of Control Line at AirVenture

Control line flying at Oshkosh actually predates the formal establishment of KidVenture. Seeking engaging, hands-on activities that would allow kids to touch and fly real airplanes, Sean Elliott—a former Junior National Stunt Champion and future EAA Vice President—teamed up with Jim Krueger and the Milwaukee Circle Masters.

In an unforgettable recruiting mission, Elliott flew EAA's full-scale Spirit of St. Louis replica directly to the annual SIG Manufacturing control line contest in Montezuma, Iowa. Landing the historic aircraft in Iowa, Elliott and Krueger approached longtime competitor Dan McEntee to recruit seasoned modelers from the St. Louis area, including Bob Arata and Jan Potts, to join the Oshkosh flight line crew. That dramatic arrival and handshake in Iowa ignited a multi-decade aeromodeling mission that has united clubs across the Midwest and beyond.

2. A Day on the Flight Line: The Anatomy of a First Flight

The daily rhythm at Pioneer Airport is a study in choreographed teamwork. Operating seven days during AirVenture week across two simultaneous turf circles, the team handles an average of 1,400 to 1,800 youth flights per week, reaching record peaks of 2,500 flights in a single convention.

The flight line operates seamlessly through two vital, collaborative roles: the Flight Instructor (Pilot) and the Pit Crew.

The Flight Experience: From Jitters to Solo Wings

1. Ground Briefing: When a young flyer steps into the circle, registration coordinators Doug & Geri MacArthur and Melissa Olson welcome the family, fit the student with safety glasses, and introduce them to their flight coach. In the circle center, the instructor explains the mechanics: tilting the wrist backward pulls the top line to climb; tilting forward lowers the elevator to descend, while keeping the arm steady against Oshkosh winds.

2. Pit Crew & Hand Launch: At the circle perimeter, the pit crew connects the battery and arms the timer. Models omit landing gear for grass durability; the pit volunteer holds the plane into the wind and delivers a smooth hand launch as the brushless motor spools to governed RPM.

3. Dual-Handle Coaching: The flight coach uses the proven dual-handle technique—placing a guiding hand over the student's on the handle to smooth out initial over-corrections and establish level flight.

4. Solo Control & Landing: Once line tension is solid, the coach steps back. The student commands climbs and descents on their own! After 2.5 minutes, the electronic timer gently ramps down power for a soft grass belly landing to cheers from parents and onlookers.

3. Airframe Innovation: From Balsa to the Eagle 1

Teaching thousands of beginners in hot, humid, and windy summer conditions demands near-indestructible equipment. The fleet evolved across five distinct eras:

  1. Club Demonstration Models (Early 1990s): Personal balsa airplanes flown by founding club members.
  2. Cox Plastic Ready-to-Fly (RTF): Commercial plastic trainers (such as the Cox PT-19) powered by Cox .049 glow engines.
  3. Bratco Skybabies & SIG Norvel .061s: Adopted Bratco Skybaby airframes backed by SIG Manufacturing with high-output Norvel .061 Big Mig engines. While the engines ran flawlessly, the high flight tempo resulted in 2 to 3 airframe casualties per day.
  4. The "Tuf Baby" (Bob Arata & Dan McEntee, 2010): Conceived on the drive back to St. Louis to solve the breakage problem (plans drawn by Art Johnson). Bob Arata built the original five Coroplast Tuf Babies in 2010; they flew thousands of flights over multiple seasons without retiring a single plane, saving countless repair hours for master technician Art Johnson.
  5. The Modern "Eagle 1" (Chris Sterner): Designed by Chairman Chris Sterner. Built from durable 4mm Coroplast, the Eagle 1 features a 30-1/4″ wingspan, 226 sq. in. wing area, brushless electric power, and rapid battery turnaround.

4. Multi-Generational Impact, Scholarships & Aviation Careers

The true measure of the KidVenture Control Line program lies in the lives it has touched across three decades:

  • Returning Flyers & Scholarships: Many children return year after year, earning pins and progressing from first-time flyers to confident modelers. Numerous alumni have gone on to receive EAA Young Eagles and AMA educational scholarships.
  • Professional Aviators & Engineers: Former student flyers frequently return to the circles wearing commercial airline captain uniforms, military flight suits, or aerospace engineering credentials, thanking volunteers for sparking their careers at KidVenture.
  • Multi-Generational Tradition: Adults who flew at KidVenture in the 1990s and 2000s now bring their own sons and daughters back to Pioneer Airport to experience the exact same magic of control line flight.

5. International Fellowship & Global Collaboration

Young attendees and families from all 50 states and over 80 countries take to the circles each week. Renowned French stunt competitors Gilbert and Véronique Béringer (founders of Beringer Aero) regularly visit to coach students and demonstrate European precision aerobatics. Aerospace pioneer Burt Rutan and EAA Vice President Sean Elliott are frequent flight line visitors, celebrating the camaraderie of model aviation.

6. Twilight Stunt, Pulse Jets & After-Hours Fun Flying

When the official KidVenture flight circles close at 3:30 PM, the energy shifts to after-hours exhibition flying:

  • Precision Aerobatics (CL Stunt): Master pilots such as Todd Lee demonstrate the full AMA precision stunt pattern (loops, inverted flight, eights, hourglasses, four-leaf clovers) against the Wisconsin sunset.
  • Pulse Jet Thunder: High-speed pulse jet demonstrations draw huge crowds with their unmistakable, deafening roar and glowing red tailpipes.
  • Evening Stunt Under the Airshow: Instructors fly sport and stunt aircraft during twilight airshows, performing precision aerobatics while WWII warbirds and fighters cruise overhead.
  • Camp Scholler Fellowship: Volunteers camp together at Camp Scholler, gathering for shared meals and the beloved Wednesday Night Volunteer BBQ.

Always Welcoming New Volunteers — Join the Flight Line!

Volunteer Benefits & Recruitment

The KidVenture control line program continually welcomes new and returning volunteers of all skill levels. Benefits include EAA AirVenture admission wristbands, parking passes, flexible 4-hour morning or afternoon shifts (leaving plenty of time to enjoy the airshow), Camp Scholler camping community camaraderie, the annual Wednesday evening volunteer BBQ, and the thrill of mentoring the next generation of aviators. Sign up at circlemasters.com or via EAA KidVenture volunteer registration.
